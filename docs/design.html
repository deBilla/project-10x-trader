1. What it has to do

Start by writing down what "done" looks like. Two lists: things it must do, and qualities it must have.

Must do

  1. Trade on its own. Buy and sell from a fixed list of 30 US stocks without a person clicking anything, during market hours.
  2. Decide with a rule, not a guess. Which stocks to own is decided by a fixed formula over daily prices — the same formula that was tested on the past.
  3. Read the news before buying. Ask Claude to scan recent headlines and refuse a purchase if the news is bad or earnings are imminent.
  4. Keep a diary. Record every decision — including "did nothing" — so any day can be explained afterwards.

Must be

  • Safe. Hard limits on position size, number of positions, and daily loss, enforced in code no AI can change. Target: zero orders outside the limits, ever.
  • Reliable. One check per hour, never two at once. If a piece breaks, that check skips gracefully; the next hour tries again.
  • Cheap. AI calls only when the rule is about to buy — a few per week.
  • Honest. Anything that decides trades must be replayable over history first. This is a requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Deliberately not doing

Real money. Short selling. Options. Crypto (built, then parked). Many users. Sub-second speed — decisions are hourly, so a slow network is fine.

A real-world limit worth knowing

US accounts under $25,000 may only make three same-day buy-then-sell round trips per five days (the PDT rule). Holding for days or weeks is unlimited. That alone rules out fast day-trading strategies for a small account, and points at the slow rule this system uses.

3. How the pieces talk to each other

There is no web API — it's one program. But the modules inside have clear boundaries, and naming them is what keeps the design honest. Each line below is one Python function or class, and the arrow is what it gives back.

# prices and the account  (market_data.py)
MarketData.get_history(symbol, days)      → daily bars
MarketData.get_account()                  → equity, cash, what we own
MarketData.submit_notional_buy(sym, $)    → order id
MarketData.close_position(sym)            → done

# the rule  (backtest/trend.py — shared by live and replay)
desired_long(bars)                        → True / False

# the AI  (news.py)
NewsSentiment.assess(symbol)              → verdict  (label, confidence, earnings?)
NewsSentiment.should_block(verdict)       → True / False, and the reason

# safety  (risk/drawdown.py)
DrawdownMonitor.check(equity)             → paused? (and liquidates if so)

# the diary  (persistence/journal.py)
Journal.record_tick(everything)           → record id

The one line to notice is desired_long. It lives in the backtest folder, and the live program imports it from there. The thing that was tested and the thing that runs are literally the same function. Keep that property; it is the whole reason the test results mean anything.

4. One hourly check, start to finish

Once an hour during market hours, the scheduler calls run_tick(). Here is what happens, in order.

1

Read the account and check the kill switch

Get today's balance. Compare it to the balance at the start of the day (stored in Redis). If we're down 10% or more, sell everything, set a "paused" flag for the rest of the day, and skip all buying below. Selling is still allowed while paused — you can always get out.

2

For each of the 30 stocks, fetch 400 days of daily bars

400 because the rule needs 100 days of history for its moving average, plus room. If fetching one stock fails, skip that stock and carry on — one bad feed should never stop the other 29.

3

Drop today's unfinished bar

While the market is open, the last "daily" bar is today's — and its "close" is really just the current price. The rule was tested on finished days only, so it must see finished days only. This step was missing for three months; see Results.

4

Ask the rule: should we own this?

desired_long(bars) answers yes or no. Then compare with whether we do own it:

Own it?Rule saysDo
yes—if it's down 20% from what we paid: sell (emergency stop)
yesnosell — the trend broke
yesyesnothing — keep holding
noyesmaybe buy — go to step 5
nononothing

Notice this compares two facts rather than reacting to an event. If the program crashes halfway through, the next hour simply recomputes both facts and does the right thing. Nothing to repair, nothing to remember.

5

Before buying: is there room, and what does the news say?

If we already hold 10 stocks, stop — no room. Otherwise ask Claude about this one stock (details in section 7). If it says "bad news" with high confidence, or "earnings are due", record BLOCKED and move on. The rule will ask again tomorrow if the trend is still there.

6

Buy — but only with cash you have

Target is 10% of the account, about $100. But you can only spend cash on hand, so take the smaller of the two, less a 2% cushion (a market order can fill slightly above the quoted price). Under $20? Skip it as too small to matter.

7

Write the diary entry

One MongoDB record: the account, every stock's yes/no and price, every action taken, and for every Claude verdict — the headlines it saw, the model used, and the prompt version. That last part matters: it means an old decision can be re-judged later with a new prompt.

6. Deep dive: the trading rule

The rule is a classic called a Donchian breakout, used by trend followers since the 1980s. In words:

  • Buy when today's close is higher than any close in the last 50 days, and the price is above its 100-day average.
  • Sell when today's close is lower than any low in the last 20 days.
  • Never take profit early. Let winners run. The only other exit is an emergency stop at 20% below the purchase price, which almost never fires because the 20-day rule usually gets there first.

Why this rule? Because it's the one the replay said made money. And why daily prices instead of minute-by-minute? Because trading rarely means paying slippage rarely, and because the PDT rule forbids fast trading on a small account anyway.

The clever bit: it reads a state, not an event

A naive version asks "did a breakout happen today?" This one asks "was the most recent breakout more recent than the most recent breakdown?" — in other words, "are we currently in an uptrend?"

entries = close > highest_high_of_last_50_days
exits   = close < lowest_low_of_last_20_days
in_trend = (date of last entry) > (date of last exit)

Two nice things fall out. The program needs no memory — restart it anywhere and it recomputes the same answer. And when Claude blocks a purchase, nothing is lost: the trend is still there tomorrow, so the rule just asks again. The purchase is delayed, not cancelled. That property turns out to matter a lot when you try to measure what the news filter costs.

7. Deep dive: the Claude SDK — what, how, why

What it is

There are two ways to use Claude from code. The plain API is a single request: send text, get text back. If you want Claude to do things — call tools, look at results, decide what to do next — you have to write that loop yourself.

The Claude Agent SDK is that loop, already written. It's the same machinery that powers the Claude Code tool, shipped as a Python library. You give it a prompt and some options; it runs the think → call a tool → read the result → think again cycle for you, and it lets you plug in tools and safety checks. Under the hood the Python library starts the Claude Code program as a helper process and talks to it.

The four ways to build an agent, and which this used

1. Plain API, write the loop yourself. 2. Plain API with its "tool runner" helper, which loops over tools you define. 3. Managed Agents, where Anthropic hosts everything. 4. The Agent SDK: the full Claude Code loop with built-in tools, MCP, and hooks, running on your own machine. This project used 4 — for reasons below.

How it's used here — two different ways

Use 1: the news check (live today). A single question, single answer. The code sends Claude a short instruction and the headlines, and asks for a one-line JSON answer.

# news.py — one-shot, no tools, one turn
from claude_agent_sdk import ClaudeAgentOptions, query

opts = ClaudeAgentOptions(system_prompt=_SYSTEM, max_turns=1, model=model)
async for msg in query(prompt=headlines_text, options=opts):
    ...collect the text...
# then parse the JSON: {"label": "bearish", "confidence": 0.6, "earnings_imminent": false, ...}

The instruction is deliberately tiny: "You are a terse financial news analyst. Judge the near-term risk for a LONG position. Respond with ONLY a JSON object." max_turns=1 means no tools, no back-and-forth — one answer and done.

Use 2: the original "AI decides" agent (built, now switched off). This is where the SDK earned its keep. Claude was given real trading tools and a safety hook.

# mcp_client.py — the full agent setup
ClaudeAgentOptions(
    model=cfg.settings.trader_model,
    system_prompt=system_prompt,                 # the strategy rules, in words
    mcp_servers={"alpaca": {"type": "http", "url": "http://alpaca-mcp:8000/mcp"}},
    allowed_tools=[                              # 4 read-only + 5 order tools
        "mcp__alpaca__get_account_info", "mcp__alpaca__get_stock_bars", ...
        "mcp__alpaca__place_stock_order", "mcp__alpaca__close_position", ...
    ],
    hooks={"PreToolUse": [
        HookMatcher(matcher="mcp__alpaca__place_.*", hooks=[risk_hook]),
        HookMatcher(matcher="mcp__alpaca__close_.*", hooks=[risk_hook]),
    ]},
)

Three things are happening in that block:

  • mcp_servers — plugs in Alpaca's official MCP server. That gives Claude "get my account", "get prices", "place order", "close position" as ready-made tools. Nobody wrote a single tool wrapper. Tools are named mcp__<server>__<tool>.
  • allowed_tools — the tools Claude may use without being asked. One honest correction to the code's own comment: per the SDK docs this list auto-approves tools, it doesn't hard-restrict Claude to them. The hard restriction is the next line.
  • hooks → PreToolUse — the safety checkpoint. Any tool whose name matches place_.* or close_.* runs through risk_hook before it executes. The hook can refuse it.

The hook — how you stop an AI doing something

The hook is an ordinary Python function. It receives the tool name and its arguments, runs the checks, and returns one of two things:

# risk/hook.py — what the SDK expects back
# to block:
return {"hookSpecificOutput": {
    "hookEventName": "PreToolUse",
    "permissionDecision": "deny",
    "permissionDecisionReason": "notional $240 exceeds cap $100 (10% of $1,000 equity)",
}}
# to allow:
return {}

When it returns "deny", the order never reaches Alpaca. Claude sees the reason as the tool's result and can try something else — but it cannot argue its way past. The SDK docs are explicit: if any hook says deny, the operation is blocked. The checks inside the hook, in order: is the day paused? · is it a short sale (not allowed)? · is the stock on the list? · does the order have a stop-loss? · is the stop too far away? · would this exceed 10 positions? · does it exceed 10% of the account? Each is one if statement, and there are 14 unit tests covering them.

Why the Agent SDK, and not the plain API?

Three real reasons, in order of importance:

  1. The hook. A safety check that runs inside the agent loop, before each tool call, is exactly what a trading bot needs. The SDK provides that as a first-class feature. (The plain API's tool-runner helper also has per-turn hooks, so this alone wasn't decisive — but with the SDK it came together with the next two.)
  2. MCP for free. Alpaca ships an MCP server. Pointing the SDK at it gave Claude nine working trading tools in one config line. With the plain API you'd write and maintain each tool's schema and handler yourself.
  3. Login. The SDK can use a Claude subscription login instead of a metered API key, which is how this personal project ran.
An honest note on that third reason

Anthropic's SDK docs say that, unless approved, third-party developers should not offer Claude.ai login to their products and should use API keys instead. This project is one person's private tool on their own subscription, which is a different situation — but if you build something like this for other people, use an API key. Also: this matters less than it sounds. The live system makes a few Claude calls a week. At API prices that's cents.

The trade-offs, honestly

  • It's heavy for the news check. Starting a helper process to ask one question is more machinery than the job needs. A plain API call with a structured-output schema would be lighter, faster, and easier to run many in parallel. The SDK was used here for consistency (one login, one library), not because the news check needs an agent.
  • Errors happen. In the four-year test, 41% of news checks failed (fetch or scoring errors). That's why the design fails open — a broken check never stops a good trade — and why the error count is printed in every report rather than hidden.
  • The big agent is switched off. Once the replay showed the "AI decides" strategy losing money, the whole tools-and-hooks setup became dormant. It's kept in the repo because the hook pattern is the most reusable idea in it.

8. Deep dive: the safety layer

Three separate layers. None depends on another, and none can be changed by anything Claude says.

Layer 1 — the settings file

SettingValueIn words
max_position_pct0.10at most 10% of the account in one stock
max_open_positions10at most 10 stocks at once
daily_drawdown_pct0.10lose 10% in a day → sell everything, stop for the day
long_onlytruenever bet on prices falling

Layer 2 — the kill switch

At every hourly check, compare the balance now to the balance at the start of the day. Down 10%? Sell everything and set a flag in Redis that stops all buying until tomorrow. The flag is keyed to today's date and expires on its own. It fires once, not every hour after.

Known wrinkle: if you change the account balance mid-day (say, top it up), the "start of day" number is stale and the switch can fire falsely. The fix is manual — clear the Redis key and restart.

Layer 3 — the hook (for the AI agent)

Described in section 7. Dormant now, but it's the answer to a question every AI-agent project eventually hits: how do you stop the model doing something you didn't sanction? Not by telling it. By intercepting it.

A bug worth learning from

The first version of the hook only recognised stop-loss prices written one way. The real Alpaca tool wrote them another way. Result: the hook refused every order, and would have done so forever. It was caught on the very first real order. Lesson: a safety check that misreads its input fails silently and completely — test it against the real thing, not your idea of the real thing.

9. Deep dive: testing before trusting

Two tools, for two different questions. The Testing page explains both properly; here is the shape.

The replay tool (backtester) answers "would this rule have made money?" It walks through years of daily prices, applying the rule one day at a time, only ever using information available that day, and charging slippage on every trade. It prints the average profit per trade, how bumpy the ride was, and — crucially — how that compares to just buying and holding.

The news-filter test answers a harder question: "what did the AI's vetoes cost?" You can't replay "was the AI right?" fairly, because Claude was trained on news from those years. So instead, run the replay twice — once with the filter, once without — and compare the totals. The difference is the price of the filter.

10. Running it

Everything runs as Docker containers on one small server: the trading program, Redis, and MongoDB. Two more (a crypto agent and the AI-agent's tool server) exist but are switched off by default.

cp .env.example .env          # add your Alpaca keys and Claude login
docker compose up -d --build  # starts the trader + redis + mongo
docker compose logs -f agent-equity | grep "trend tick"

The settings folder is mounted into the container, so changing a number in a YAML file and restarting is enough — no rebuild. Tests run with plain pytest and need no network: 73 of them, covering the rule, the replay, the hook, the kill switch, and the news parsing.

11. Build order, with checks

If you were building this from scratch, this is the order that lets you verify each piece before the next depends on it. It is also, roughly, the order it was actually built — except the replay tool came far too late.

1

Read prices and your account

Wrap the broker's library: get daily bars for one stock, get your balance. Nothing else yet.

Check: print 400 days of Apple and your fake balance. If a split shows up as a fake crash, you forgot to ask for split-adjusted prices.
2

Write the rule as one pure function

desired_long(bars) → True/False. No network, no side effects. Put it somewhere the replay tool will import it from too.

Check: feed it a steadily rising series, expect True; a falling one, expect False. Write these as tests now.
3

Build the replay tool — before the bot

Walk day by day. Decide at each close, act at the next open. Charge slippage both ways. Print average profit per trade, and buy-and-hold for the same stocks.

Check: run it on your rule over 4 years. If profit per trade is negative, stop and rethink the rule. If it doesn't beat buy-and-hold, be suspicious. Do not build the bot until this passes.
4

The hourly loop

Scheduler → for each stock: fetch bars, drop today's unfinished bar, ask the rule, compare with holdings, act. No AI yet. No safety layer yet either — but no real money, so that's fine for a day.

Check: run one tick by hand. Confirm it buys what the rule says and nothing else. Confirm a second run does nothing (it's already holding). Confirm exits happen after the close, not mid-day.
5

Safety limits and the kill switch

Position cap, position count, daily loss limit. Store the day's start balance in Redis, keyed by date.

Check: set the loss limit to 0.1% and watch it fire. Confirm buying stops and selling still works. Reset the key.
6

The diary

One MongoDB record per tick with everything — especially the "did nothing" ticks.

Check: after a quiet day, can you explain from the diary alone why nothing happened? If not, you're not recording enough.
7

The news check

Fetch 48 hours of headlines. Send them to Claude with a tiny prompt asking for JSON. Parse it defensively. Cache per stock for two hours. On any error, return "don't block". Record the headlines and prompt version with every verdict.

Check: unplug the network and confirm the bot still buys. Feed it a made-up "CEO arrested" headline and confirm it blocks.
8

Test the news check the hard way

Run the replay twice, with and without the filter, over the same years. Compare average profit per trade. Count how many blocks landed on losing trades versus the loss rate you'd get by blocking at random.

Check: if the filtered run is worse, raise the confidence threshold or switch it off. If it's about the same, it's harmless — keep it or not, but don't build on it.
9

Compare live to the replay — every week

The step that was missing. Count your trades per week, note what time of day exits happen, note how long you hold. Compare each to the replay's numbers.

Check: if live trades 2–3× as often as the replay, or exits cluster mid-day, live is not running the rule you tested. Find out why before anything else.
If you only remember one step

Step 3 before step 4. The replay tool is not a nice-to-have you add later. It is the thing that decides whether the bot is allowed to exist.
