How we know what works

1. The replay tool

A backtest is a replay of the past. You take years of daily prices, walk through them one day at a time, apply your rule at each step exactly as the bot would, and total up what would have happened. Simple idea, easy to get wrong. Five rules keep it honest:

1

Never peek at tomorrow

A decision made at Monday's close is acted on at Tuesday's opening price — never at Monday's close, which you couldn't have got. Break this rule and every strategy looks brilliant, because it's quietly cheating.

2

Charge for every trade

Assume you buy slightly above the price and sell slightly below it — 0.05% each way, 0.10% per round trip. This one line is what exposed the first strategy: it earned about 0.02% per trade and paid 0.10% to do it.

3

Fix the prices for stock splits

When a company splits its shares 10-for-1, the raw price drops 90% overnight with nothing actually lost. Unadjusted, that looks like a crash and wrecks the replay. An early run showed Nvidia "losing" 78% this way before it was caught.

4

Don't trade before the rule has enough history

The rule needs 100 days of prices for its average. The replay refuses to trade for the first 100 days rather than pretending.

5

Always compare with doing nothing

For each stock, and for the whole basket, print what buying once and holding would have made. If your clever rule doesn't beat that, it isn't clever. The tool ends with a blunt line: "positive edge after costs" or "NO edge".

2. What the replay said

Over 1,500 days (about four years) across all 30 stocks, charging costs, putting 10% of the account in each trade:

256 trades   55% won   average +5.52% per trade   $2.73 won per $1 lost
total return +290%   worst dip 11%   just holding: +176%
VERDICT: POSITIVE edge (after modeled costs)

In plain terms: the rule made money, it beat just holding the stocks, and it did so with a much smaller worst-case dip (11% versus the roughly 30% an ordinary stock basket fell in 2022). That's the claim this project makes — similar gains, much less stomach-churning.

Two cautions. The "+290%" compounds 256 small trades at 10% size each, while "+176%" is for holding 100% — so they're not like-for-like per stock, only for the whole approach. And this replay was on famous, still-existing companies during mostly good years. See Results for what it does and doesn't prove.

And the first strategy?

Same tool, same years: −0.08% per trade, $0.45 won per $1 lost, roughly −50% overall. It was removed from the live program the same day.

3. Why the AI needed a different test

The trading rule could be replayed because it's just arithmetic on prices. The news filter can't be, for two reasons:

Claude knows the ending. It was trained on text from those years. Ask it about Nvidia in early 2023 and it isn't predicting — it's remembering. Any replay that asks "was the AI right?" is rigged in the AI's favour.

News moves prices fast. By the time a headline is read and scored, the price has already reacted. A daily replay can't see that at all.

So instead of asking "is it right?", the test asks "what did its decisions cost?" That question can be answered, because the filter can only do one thing: block a purchase.

4. Getting the question right

The filter can only say no. That makes the two kinds of mistake very unequal:

  • Blocking a trade that would have won costs real money — that trade averaged +5.5%.
  • Failing to block a trade that lost costs nothing extra — you're simply back where you'd be without the filter.

So "how accurate is it?" is the wrong question — it counts both mistakes equally. The right question is: of the trades it blocked, what share would have lost money? That's called block precision.

The pass mark isn't 50%

If you blocked trades at random, you'd land on a loser exactly as often as the rule loses — 44.7% of the time here. So a filter has to beat 44.7% to be doing anything at all. The pass mark moves with the strategy's win rate, which is why the tool calculates it fresh each run instead of hard-coding it.

5. The method: run it twice

Same four years, same replay code, run two times. Once with the news filter switched off. Once with it on — using the real prompt, the real thresholds, and only headlines published before each decision. Then compare the totals.

One trap the tool avoids

The obvious way to price the filter is to add up what the blocked trades would have earned. That number is badly wrong, because of how the rule works: a blocked purchase isn't cancelled, it's delayed. The trend is still there tomorrow, the rule asks again, and it usually buys a few days later — catching most of the same move. In the real run, every single blocked trade was bought later.

So the only honest cost is the difference in the totals between the two runs. The report labels every block "re-entered later" or "leg abandoned" so nobody misreads it.

Verdict: roughly nothing, possibly slightly good

The filter changed the average trade by 0.03 percentage points. Its blocks did land on losers more often than chance (64% vs 45%) — but on 11 examples, there's about a 1-in-6 chance of doing that well by luck. Too few to trust. It was kept because it's harmless and cheap. It was deliberately not expanded on the strength of eleven data points.

The number I didn't expect

111 of 271 checks failed — the headline fetch or the AI call errored. Every failure was treated as "don't block", by design. That means for a large slice of this test, the rule was trading with no filter at all. It reframes what was measured, and it teaches something general: for an AI feature in a system that does real things, the decision about what happens when it breaks matters more than the prompt.

7. The known flaw

Claude was trained on news from these years. It has, in some sense, read how they turned out. That makes the filter look better than it really is. So:

  • A bad result is a floor — reality is at least that bad.
  • A good result proves nothing.

The tool prints this warning on every run. A near-zero result under conditions that flatter the filter should be read as "near zero at best".

A useful side effect

Every Claude verdict is cached on disk, keyed by the prompt version and model. Change the prompt, and every cached answer is thrown out and re-scored. That means the test doubles as a regression check: edit the prompt, re-run, and see whether the numbers moved.
