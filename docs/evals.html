03 — Evals

Measuring the strategy, then measuring the model

Two harnesses, built months apart, for two different questions. The first decided what the system trades. The second decided how much of the language model to keep. Both returned answers that cost their author something.

The backtester — five disciplines

A walk-forward bar simulator over historical OHLCV. It produces closed trades with net returns, and a metrics layer turns those into expectancy, profit factor, compounded return, max drawdown and an annualised per-trade Sharpe. Five rules give the output its credibility.

01

No lookahead

A signal computed at bar i's close is acted on at bar i+1's open. A pending variable carries the decision across the boundary, so there is no code path in which a decision can consult the bar it executes on.

02

Modelled costs

5 bps slippage per side by default, applied to entry and exit, commission zero for Alpaca. That gives a 10 bps round-turn drag subtracted from every trade's gross return. This single parameter is what exposed the original strategy: it had roughly 2 bps of gross edge to pay a 10 bps bill.

03

Split and dividend adjustment

History is fetched with adjustment=ALL. Without it a stock split appears as a price crash — an unadjusted NVDA showed a fake −78% and silently corrupted an entire run before it was caught. Any backtest spanning a split without adjustment is measuring a fiction.

04

Warmup

Both simulators compute the longest indicator window they need and refuse to trade before it. A 100-day SMA cannot produce a signal on day 40, and a harness that pretends otherwise reports trades that could never have happened.

05

Compare against buy-and-hold

Printed per symbol and for the portfolio. A strategy that underperforms simply holding the same basket has not found an edge, however good its Sharpe looks in isolation. The runner ends with a blunt verdict line — positive expectancy after costs, or "NO edge". That line is what killed version one.

Why the LLM needed a different harness

The trend engine was backtestable because its rules are deterministic functions of price. News sentiment is not. You cannot replay a language model over history and call the result a signal test, for two reasons that compound:

The model knows the future. It was trained on text published after the dates you are replaying. Ask it about NVDA in February 2023 and it is not predicting, it is remembering.

News alpha decays faster than the harness can resolve. By the time a headline is fetched and scored, the move is priced. A daily-bar simulator cannot represent that at all.

So the question was reframed. Not "can this predict?" — which is unanswerable offline — but "what do its interventions cost?" That question is answerable, because the component's only power is to block.

Framing the question correctly

The filter can only veto. That asymmetry determines the metric entirely:

  • A false positive kills a trade drawn from a +5.5%-expectancy distribution. That costs real money.
  • A false negative merely returns you to the unfiltered baseline. That costs nothing at all.

Accuracy is therefore the wrong measure — it weights both errors equally when they are not remotely equal. The right one is block precision: of the blocks that can be priced against a baseline trade, what share landed on a trade that would have lost?

The bar is not 50%

Blocking at random already scores the baseline loss rate — the strategy wins 55.3% of its trades, so 44.7% of random blocks land on a loser. A filter has to beat that to carry any information at all. Note the bar moves with the strategy's win rate, which is why the harness computes it per run rather than hard-coding it; a naive framing grades against a coin flip and declares victory at 55%.

Method: two arms, one simulator

Identical history, identical code path. The only difference between the arms is a single injected callable.

Two engineering details make this work at all. The veto rule is not reimplemented for the harness — the eval is handed the production should_block and fetch_window methods, so it cannot silently drift from what runs live. And because the simulator is a tight synchronous loop while scoring is async and network-bound, each symbol's simulation runs in a worker thread and hands scoring requests back to the event loop.

That last part hides a deadlock the code documents and sidesteps: the simulations get their own thread pool rather than sharing the default, because otherwise every worker eventually becomes a simulation blocked on a headline fetch that cannot get a thread.

Keeping the future out

Daily bars are stamped with a session date, but the decision is taken at the close. The harness maps each bar to the moment its close was observed — 21:00 UTC, chosen as the conservative bound through daylight saving — and windows headlines to [close − 48h, close). Nothing published after the decision can reach the model, and the veto callback is documented as being invoked at the decision bar's close for exactly this reason.

Attribution — the part everyone gets wrong

Having collected the blocks, the obvious move is to add up what those trades would have earned and call it the cost of the filter. That number is badly wrong, and the reason traces directly back to the state-read design of desired_long().

Because the trend signal reports a state rather than an event, a blocked entry is retried the next day while the leg stays valid. A veto therefore delays an entry rather than cancelling it, and the filtered arm usually gets into the same move a few days late, recapturing most of it. In the actual run, every single priced block was re-entered later.

The only honest measure

The filter's cost is the expectancy and return delta between the two arms — not the sum of the blocked trades' returns. The report labels each block re-entered later or leg abandoned precisely so this cannot be misread.

The attribution pass also handles a counting trap. A block on Monday is followed by retries on Tuesday, Wednesday and so on for as long as the leg lives. That is one blocked opportunity, not four, so the harness marks window starts and reports those — otherwise the headline "blocks" figure inflates with every day the trend persists. In this run: 11 blocked entries across 15 veto-days.

Verdict — roughly neutral

A delta of +0.03 percentage points of expectancy on 11 blocks. Precision beat its base rate — 63.6% against 44.7% — but at n=11 the probability of scoring 7-or-better purely by chance is about 0.17. That is suggestive and nowhere near significant.

The defensible reading is that the veto is approximately free, possibly slightly positive, and nothing to build on yet. It was kept, because free is acceptable and the plumbing has other uses. It was deliberately not expanded on the strength of eleven data points.

The error rate deserves its own note

111 of 271 consultations errored — headline fetch failures and scoring failures — and every one of them failed open. That is not a bug being confessed; it is the designed behaviour working. But it reframes what the run measured: for much of it, the system was trading its deterministic strategy completely unfiltered.

The design lesson generalises. For an LLM component in a system with real consequences, the error path is not an edge case to handle later — here it was the plurality path, and the decision about which way it fails is a more consequential design choice than the prompt.

Stated contamination

The harness prints this caveat on every single run, and it belongs here too. The model has training knowledge of what happened after these dates. That biases the filter toward looking good.

So the results are asymmetric in what they can support:

  • A poor result is a lower bound on how poor it really is.
  • A good result is not proof of an edge.

Which means the honest conclusion from a near-zero result is not "the filter is neutral" but "the filter is neutral at best, measured under conditions that favour it".

The cache is the regression test

Verdicts are cached on disk keyed by symbol|day|lookback|prompt_version|model. Changing the prompt or the model invalidates every verdict by design — that is the regression signal, not an inconvenience. Re-running after changing only the report costs nothing, which is what makes iterating on the harness affordable.
