This is the classic shape of trend following, and it explains every design choice upstream. The rule is right only slightly more than half the time. All of the profit comes from winners being about twice as big as losers — which only happens because there is no "take profit" point, and the exit is a trailing 20-day low.

Two consequences. Capping winners would destroy the edge, which is why there's no profit target. And a rule like this trades rarely: 256 trades across 30 stocks in four years is about two per stock per year. Long stretches where nothing happens are the rule working, not the rule broken.

Three months of real running

Everything above is a replay. In September 2026 the obvious question finally got asked: the bot has been running since June — what has it actually done? This is the most valuable data on this site, because it's the only part nobody could have fitted after the fact.

The bot underperformed a do-nothing basket of its own stocks by about 5 percentage points, and the S&P 500 by about 4. Against a replay implying something like 39% a year, that's not a rounding error.

The cause was a bug, not the rule

The live trade log didn't look like the replay's. Three clues, all pointing the same way:

  • It sold and re-bought the same stock on the same day. For the S&P 500 fund it went: sell 29 July, buy 29 July, sell 30 July, buy 4 Aug, sell 1 Sept, buy 9 Sept, sell 10 Sept. Same pattern on Apple, AMD and others.
  • Every sale happened in the middle of the trading day. All 21 of them, between 9am and 2pm New York time. None after the market closed.
  • It traded almost three times as often as the replay predicted. 44 buy-or-sell actions in 93 days, where the replay suggests about 16.

What was happening: the rule is meant to look at each day's closing price. But when the program fetched "daily" prices during the trading day, the last entry was today's — unfinished, with the current price sitting where the close should be. A brief morning dip below the 20-day low made the rule say "sell". An hour later, with the price back up, it said "buy". Two trades, two lots of costs, no gain.

The number that makes it obvious

The ten stocks the bot was holding — Apple, Bank of America, Salesforce, Disney, Johnson & Johnson, JPMorgan, Mastercard, Microsoft, Nvidia, Visa — rose an average of 16.8% over those three months. Simply holding exactly what it held would have made 16.8%. It made 0.82%. The stock picking worked. The constant selling and re-buying ate all of it.

Fixed on 12 September (commit 334750e): drop today's unfinished bar before asking the rule. A test now reproduces the bug — feed it a mid-day dip, confirm the old code sells and the new code holds.

The real lesson isn't the two-line fix. It's that nothing was checking whether the live bot behaved like the replay. A project whose whole point is "measure before you trust" ran a different strategy from the one it tested, for three months, and only noticed because someone sat down to write documentation.

What was achieved, engineering-wise

One rule, two uses

What was tested is what runs

The live program imports the trading rule from the replay tool. There is no separate "live" version that can drift away from the tested one.

AI on a leash

An AI that can't exceed its job

No prompt trick, bad answer, or malformed reply can open a position, raise a limit, or skip a stop. Those code paths don't exist.

A diary you can re-judge

Every decision is explainable later

Each record stores the headlines Claude saw and which prompt version judged them, so old decisions can be re-scored with a new prompt.

Tests that can say no

Both tools overruled their author

A test that has never told you something you didn't want to hear isn't a test — it's decoration.

Two ideas here travel well beyond trading. The hook: stop an AI doing something by intercepting the action, not by asking. And the run-it-twice test: price an AI feature that can only say "no" by comparing totals with and without it, rather than grading its answers.

What the numbers do and don't prove

Supported

That the trend rule made money after costs in this replay, over these stocks and years. That the first strategy did not, by a margin far too large to be luck. That the news filter costs roughly nothing. That the safety limits hold — no limit has ever been breached by an AI action in either version.

Not supported

That this makes money going forward — and this is no longer merely untested. Ninety-three days of real running returned +0.82% against +5.70% for holding. That window is too short to condemn the rule (and a bug explains most of it), but it's real, it's out-of-sample, and it points the wrong way. Also not supported: that the news filter predicts anything; that the "Sharpe" figure is comparable to a fund's (it's calculated differently); that the rule works outside big US companies in a rising market, which is the only place it's been tested.

The gap between those two boxes is the honest position. This project produced good evidence about which of its own parts were worth building, and weak evidence about whether the result makes money. Those are different claims, and the second needs years of forward data, not a better replay.

What's still wrong with it

Listed plainly, because a write-up that only lists strengths is an advert.

About the testing

  • The replay used famous companies that still exist, during good years — the kindest possible sample. A proper test needs companies that went bust, and a bad year or two.
  • The AI test's flaw (Claude remembers the news) can't be fixed with more computing. Only running forward on dates Claude hasn't seen escapes it.
  • The "Sharpe ratio" is calculated per trade, not per day. Don't compare it to published fund numbers.

About the live program

  • Nothing compares live behaviour to the replay. This is the gap that let the timing bug run for three months. Trades per week, hold length and time-of-day of exits are all cheap to check, and none were.
  • No automatic stop-loss at the broker. Fractional shares can't have one, so exits depend on the program running. If it's down over a weekend and a stock gaps lower on Monday, nothing catches it.
  • Changing the balance mid-day can trip the kill switch falsely, and recovery is manual.
  • No check that the hourly timer actually fires. A bug once left it only running on restart, and nothing would catch that automatically today.
  • The live program doesn't cancel its own stale orders; the old AI version did.
  • 28 buy orders failed with "insufficient buying power" — the 2% cash cushion isn't quite enough on some fills.

About the setup

  • One program, one account. No plan for more, and no need.
  • The AI-agent's tool server can't currently be built from its upstream repository.
  • Beating buy-and-hold consistently is hard. The realistic win is similar return with much less pain, plus a way to keep checking.

What's next

Confirm the fix worked. Over the coming weeks: trades should drop to roughly 16 per 93 days, sales should happen after the close instead of mid-day, and holds should lengthen toward the replay's ten weeks. If those don't move, something else is wrong.

Then close the live gaps above — the weekly live-vs-replay check first, because it's the one that would have caught this.

Then earn the right to trust the rule. Re-run the replay on a stock list that includes companies that failed, across a bad year, in rolling windows. The current numbers are a hypothesis, not a result.

Leave the news filter alone. The test says it's worth about nothing, measured generously. The right response is not a better prompt — it's to stop spending attention there until something changes.

In one sentence

A project whose most useful output was a test that told its author to delete what he'd just built — twice — and then a third look that found the live version wasn't running what was tested.
