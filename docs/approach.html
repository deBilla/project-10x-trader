Approach

Three rules, and everything they threw away

The design only makes sense once you know the three rules it follows. Each one was learned by getting it wrong first, and each one deleted code that was already running.

The three rules

1

Don't trade a rule you haven't tested

Before any rule is allowed to place a real order, it has to be written as plain code and replayed over years of past prices, including the costs of trading. If the replay says it loses money, it does not ship. No exceptions, no "let's just try it for a week".

This sounds obvious. Most hobby trading bots skip it, because writing the replay tool is more work than writing the bot. Here, the replay tool is the boss: when it said the first strategy lost money, the strategy was removed the same day.

2

Only give the AI jobs it's good at — and only let it say no

Language models like Claude are excellent at reading text and poor at arithmetic over long lists of numbers. So all the number-crunching (averages, highs, lows) is done by ordinary code, and Claude only ever sees words: news headlines.

The second half matters more. Claude's answer can only block a purchase the rule already wanted to make. There is no path in the code where a Claude answer causes a buy. Even if Claude said "this stock is definitely going up!", the program would treat that exactly like "no opinion".

Why so strict? Because something that can only say "no" has exactly one way to hurt you — blocking a good trade — and that one thing can be measured. Something that can also say "yes" has many ways to hurt you, most of them hard to measure.

3

Keep the safety limits in plain code the AI can't reach

How much to put in one stock (10% of the account). How many stocks at once (10). Which stocks are allowed (the 30 on the list). When to sell everything and stop for the day (a 10% loss). All of these are ordinary settings in a text file, enforced by ordinary Python.

In the first version, where Claude placed orders itself, every order passed through a checkpoint that could refuse it before it reached the broker. Claude could not see the checkpoint, could not talk to it, and could not change its settings. It is the difference between telling a new employee "please don't spend more than $100" and giving them a card with a $100 limit.

What got thrown away, and why

The "AI decides" strategy

It watched for stocks that had dropped sharply and bet on a bounce, checking every five minutes. The replay showed a loss of about 0.08% per trade. That sounds tiny, and it is — that's the point. Each trade earned roughly 0.02% before costs, and cost about 0.10% in slippage to get in and out. A strategy that trades often and earns little per trade will always lose to its own costs. No better prompt fixes that.

Options

The conservative options strategy needs you to own 100 shares of a company — around $30,000 a position. The other kind (buying options) has no price history to replay against, has a 6.5% built-in cost every time you trade, and loses money for most people most of the time. Failed rule 1 before anything was built.

Using news to decide what to buy

The tempting version of the news feature: read a headline, buy the stock. It was never built, for a reason that has nothing to do with AI quality. By the time a headline has been fetched and read, the price has already moved — professional systems react in milliseconds. Being late and buying is expensive. Being late and declining to buy costs nothing. So the AI was only ever given the second job.

Crypto

Fully built and switched off. The paper-trading broker simply doesn't fill crypto orders — every type was tried and all sat unfilled forever. The code is fine; the test environment isn't. It's parked behind a switch rather than deleted.

A note on cost

People worry about AI costs in a project like this. They're not the issue. Claude is only asked about a stock when the rule is about to buy it — a few times a week, not every minute. The entire four-year test of the news filter took 149 Claude calls.

The cost that actually decided the design was slippage — the small loss on every trade — because it turned a rule that was barely profitable into one that reliably lost. If you're building something like this, measure your trading costs before you measure your AI bill.
