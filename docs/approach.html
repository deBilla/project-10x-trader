Project 10X Overview Approach Design Evals Results

01 — Approach

Three rules, and the log of everything they killed

The codebase looks arbitrary until you know the rules it follows. All three were learned the expensive way, and each one deleted working code that had already shipped.

The three rules

01

Never trade an unvalidated edge

The decision logic must be expressible as code, replayable over history, and measured with modelled costs before it touches an account. This is why the backtester is not an afterthought bolted on at the end — it is the component that has final say over what ships.

The corollary is harsh, and it was applied without hedging. The original mean-reversion strategy was backtested, came back with negative expectancy, and was removed from the live path the same day. Not tuned. Not given another window. Removed.

02

The model goes only where it has edge — and only as a veto

Language models are unreliable at arithmetic over price series and genuinely good at synthesising prose. So indicators are computed in pandas and never by the model, and Claude reads headlines, which is a language task.

The second half of the rule matters more than the first. Claude's output can only subtract — block an entry the deterministic signal already wanted. It can never add one. A bullish verdict has exactly the same effect as a neutral one: the control flow has no branch that makes a trade more likely.

That asymmetry is not stylistic. It is what makes the component measurable, because a thing that can only block has exactly one way to hurt you — and that single failure mode is what the eval prices.

03

Risk is enforced in code, beneath the decision layer

Position caps, open-position counts, the symbol whitelist and the daily drawdown kill-switch are read from YAML at startup and enforced by plain Python. In the original LLM mode they were enforced by a PreToolUse hook the model could not reach around: the hook intercepted every order tool call before it reached the server and could deny it outright.

No model output has ever been able to raise a limit, and the risk layer has no path to the model — the isolation runs in both directions.

What got rejected, and why

Four ideas were tried seriously and dropped. The reasons are more useful than the strategies.

Intraday mean-reversion

RSI extremes plus a Bollinger touch, on 5-minute bars, with the LLM deciding. Measured at −0.08% per trade and profit factor 0.45; the looser "active" variant lost about half the account.

The diagnosis matters: net expectancy was −8 bps against a modelled 10 bps round-turn cost, so the gross edge was around +2 bps. The strategy was not wrong so much as too small to survive its own transaction costs. Churn was the killer, and no amount of prompt engineering addresses churn.

Small-account options

Covered calls require 100-share lots, which is $30,000-plus per position — impossible on a $1,000 account. Buying options instead runs into three walls at once: no historical option chains to backtest against, bid/ask spreads around 6.5%, and a negative base rate for long premium. It failed the first rule on the "unbacktestable" clause alone.

News as a directional signal

The tempting version of the news component reads a headline and decides to buy. It was not built, for a reason that is structural rather than empirical: by the time an RSS item or an EDGAR filing has been fetched, deduplicated, and scored, the move it describes is tens of seconds old and already priced. Headline-driven entries are a latency game this system deliberately does not enter.

What remained was the veto — the one use of news where being slightly late costs nothing, because you are declining to act rather than racing to.

Crypto

Built, containerised, and paused. Alpaca's paper environment does not reliably fill crypto orders — market, limit, notional and quantity variants were all confirmed sitting unfilled. The agent is gated behind a Compose profile rather than deleted, because the code is fine and only the venue is the problem.

A note on what "cheap" means

The standard framing for LLM cost control is tokens per day. That framing is available here and it is not the binding constraint. Claude is consulted only on breakout candidates — a few times a week, not thirty times an hour — so the whole 1,500-day eval cost 149 model calls.

For an account this size the dominant costs are elsewhere: market data subscriptions, spread and slippage, and the box it runs on. Slippage in particular is the cost that actually decided the architecture, since it is what turned a marginally positive gross edge into a reliably negative net one. Any future cost NFR on this system should be denominated in basis points against equity, not dollars per day of inference.
