A personal project · fake money only

A trading bot that tested itself — and failed its own test

I built a program that buys and sells US stocks on its own, with Claude reading the news for it. Then I built the tools to check whether it actually worked. Twice, those tools told me to throw away what I'd just built. This site explains what was built, how, and what the numbers really say — in plain words.

What this is, in one paragraph

A program that runs on a small home server. Once an hour during market hours it looks at 30 well-known US stocks, decides which ones to own using a simple, old-fashioned rule, asks Claude to check the news before buying anything, and places orders with an online broker. It uses a paper-trading account, meaning real prices but fake money. No real cash has ever been at risk.

It is about 3,100 lines of Python. The part that decides what to buy is about 140 of those lines and contains no AI at all.

The one idea to take away

The interesting thing here is not the trading bot. It is the two testing tools that were built alongside it. Both were built to check whether a part of the system was worth keeping. Both came back with "no". Both times, the answer was acted on.

The story, in four steps

1

Build the obvious thing deleted

The first version did what you would expect an "AI trading bot" to do. Every few minutes it handed Claude a list of numbers about each stock and asked it to decide: buy, sell, or wait. Claude placed the orders itself, through a set of tools, with a safety layer checking every order. It worked, in the sense that it ran and never broke a limit.

Problem nobody had checked whether the strategy made money
2

Build a way to check the turning point

So I built a backtester: a program that replays the last few years of prices and asks "if I had followed this exact rule, what would have happened?" It said the first strategy lost money on every trade — about half the account gone over the replay. The reason was simple: each trade earned a tiny bit, but the cost of buying and selling ate more than it earned.

The same tool found that a much simpler rule — buy when a stock hits a 50-day high, sell when it drops to a 20-day low — made money: about 5.5% per trade on average. So the live program was rewritten around that rule, and Claude was taken out of the buy/sell decision completely.

Result strategy replaced · AI removed from decisions
3

Give the AI one small job, then test that too kept

Claude came back with one narrow task: before the program buys a stock, read the last two days of headlines about it and say "don't" if the news is clearly bad or earnings are due. It can block a purchase. It can never cause one.

Then I built a second test to find out what that filter was worth. Answer: almost nothing. Over a four-year replay it changed the average trade by 0.03 percentage points. It blocked 11 purchases, 7 of which would have lost money — better than chance, but on 11 examples that could easily be luck.

Result kept because it's harmless · not expanded
4

Look at what it actually did bug found

Writing this site prompted the obvious question: the bot has been running for three months — how did it do? Badly. It made +0.8% in 93 days. Simply buying its 30 stocks and doing nothing would have made +5.7%.

The cause was a bug, not the strategy. The rule was meant to look at each day's closing price. But during the trading day, the program was looking at the current price and treating it as a close. So a brief mid-day dip would make it sell — and an hour later, when the price recovered, it would buy back in. Paying the cost of a trade both ways, for nothing. The ten stocks it was holding actually rose 16.8% over those three months; the bot captured almost none of it.

Result right stocks, wrong timing · fixed and redeployed

Before you believe any number here

Every figure on this site comes from fake money and from replays of the past. The "4-year replay" was done on famous companies that are still around, during years when the stock market mostly went up — the kindest possible test. The AI test has a known flaw: Claude was trained on news from those years, so it may "remember" how things turned out.

Nothing here shows that this system makes money. What it shows is which parts of it were worth building, which is a much smaller claim, and the only one I'm making.
